Across the whole week: can this work stay sustainable?

Preparation, travel, gaps between bookings, messages, records and acquisition may sit around paid coaching. Ask what gives the coach energy, what drains it, and which work is unpaid. This is a cross-cutting condition—not the final stage of a client relationship.

Same profession.
Different realities.

Who gym-based and online coaches are, what they do, and which goals may shape their work. Selected Polish and international examples—not a representative portrait.

Online describes delivery—not where the athlete trains.

An online coach can support someone training in a gym. The same coach can work in person, remotely, or both. Separate format, working arrangement, specialization, and depth of support.

Who are we actually comparing?

Gym-employed / floor coach

Employer organizes access, policies and shifts. May combine inductions, member support, classes or PT.

Working model to verify; exact duties/contracts differ by gym.

Independent gym-based PT

Own client relationships, in-person sessions, and possible facility fee/rental or revenue sharing.

Arrangement is a hypothesis to ask about; it was not established for OPTENSE trainers.

Studio / small-group coach

Deliver a class or small-group experience with shared space, equipment and timetable.

Published examples: OPTENSE and Barry’s; employment status not established.

Remote 1:1 coach

Program, review reports/video, message, check in and adjust between client-led sessions.

ELT publishes check-ins and portal support; real coach workload is unknown.

Program seller

Provide a plan with limited or no ongoing support.

ELT explicitly distinguishes program-only from a coaching relationship.

Hybrid coach

Mix live sessions and remote programming/support for the same or different clients.

Comparison model to investigate; do not assume every sampled provider offers this.

Questions for the next conversations

  1. Walk me through yesterday: sessions, prep, check-ins, admin, travel and unpaid support.
  2. Who owns the client, chooses tools, sets the fee and approves changes?
  3. What happened the last time a client missed a session or stopped responding?
  4. What makes a client renew or leave—and what evidence do you have?
  5. What do you want your work to look like in a year? What would you refuse to sacrifice?
  6. If you coach in both formats, what changes between them?
What this means for Barbl—still hypotheses

Live-session work may favor quick recall, logging and substitutions; remote work may favor context, review and communication. Hybrid coaching may need continuity across both. Program sellers may not need an ongoing attention workflow. Establish who controls tools and budget before assuming who pays.

Proposed Now / Next / Later—not an agreed roadmap

Now: training log and programs, coach workspace, measurements/photos, basic attention triggers. Next: nutrition estimates, wearable context, goal-specific reports, coach communities, smart scales. Separate consent for health/body photos; control over sharing and deletion; useful workflows without integrations; clear boundaries around illness, pain, and medical conditions; community density; progressive disclosure of settings. 03 / Research from the beginning

Observe first.
Choose later.

Start broad enough to compare real jobs, then narrow around a repeated difficulty. No segment or territory is validated yet.

01 / EPISODESCollect real situationsInterview coaches and athletes about their last workout, check-in, missed week, or group interaction.
02 / WORKAROUNDSTrace current behaviorTools, decisions, handoffs, time, current spend, and consequences—with artifacts when permitted.
03 / COMPAREFind a repeated jobFrequency, urgency, impact, dissatisfaction, reachability, payer, and feasibility.
04 / TESTTry one promiseUse a small prototype or manual service. Define the behavior that supports or contradicts it.

Suggested first sample

Coaches

Start with 5–10 online coaches, including program-only, check-in-heavy, video-review, and hybrid approaches. Vary client load and tools. This sample size follows the deck’s proposal, not a representativeness claim.

Athletes

Include self-directed athletes and coached athletes; vary experience, workout context, and recent interruptions. Compare payer, end user, and decision maker rather than treating them as one person.

Community organizers

Explore an existing coach group and a local gym group if community remains promising. Study active participation and the cold-start problem before treating a feed as retention.

Competition by job

Compare strength loggers, coaching suites, informal spreadsheets/chat, and relevant communities. The old challenge competitor list is a starting artifact inside Challenges, not a complete broader-market review.

Questions to ask before showing the concept

  3. How often does it happen? When is it not a problem?
  4. What have you already tried or paid for?
  5. Who decides, who acts, and who pays?
  6. What would make changing your current workflow difficult?

Central research question

Who is already running challenges — and what's painful enough for them to pay to fix?

We are not choosing the user upfront. We'll investigate existing behaviors, payments, recurring demand, friction, and switching motivation across segments before defining the MVP.

Research directions

Navigate by topic. Each section starts with what we know, what is still a hypothesis, and what we need to find out.

03 / Proof

What evidence do we have?

Collect actual offers, product examples, complaints, and interviews with source links.

04 / Product

Where's the opportunity?

Identify an underserved customer, distinctive value proposition, and requirements for a first release.

Working principles

Behavior before opinions

Prioritize evidence of real challenges launched, participants enrolled, and money changing hands.

Separate fact from guesswork

Every finding should have a source and an evidence label. A published offer is not proof of sales.

Look for repetition

A customer who runs challenges monthly may have a more urgent workflow than someone trying one once.

Test app friction

An organizer might want an app while participants may resist downloading another one.

05 / Existing workflow

From idea
to cohort.

A provisional participant journey to check against actual organizers. These steps are not yet verified as universal.

01 / LAUNCHPromoteSocial post, stories, invite, or landing page
02 / REVENUERegister & payPayment link, form, membership tool
03 / DELIVERYRun togetherDaily content, workouts, group chat, check-ins
04 / RETENTIONWrap & repeatResults, testimonials, next cohort, upsell

Organizer pain to verify

  • Setting up the same structure repeatedly
  • Tracking who's paid and who's joined
  • Answering repeated questions in chat
  • Keeping participants engaged
  • Tracking participation and progress

Participant friction to verify

  • Too many different links or tools
  • Confusion about what to do each day
  • Missing messages or assignments
  • Low accountability after joining
  • Reluctance to install another app

07 / Customer discovery

Talk to
organizers.

We need real behavioral details, not just opinions on the app concept. Target: 10–15 conversations across at least three segments.

Interview rule

Ask about the last challenge they actually ran.

What did they do, when did they do it, what did they use, where did money flow, and what went wrong?

Core interview questions

  1. When was your most recent challenge?
  2. How many participants joined?
  3. What did you charge, if anything?
  4. Where did participants come from?
  5. How did you handle registration and payment?
  6. What tools handled content, chat and check-ins?
  7. What was unexpectedly difficult?
  8. Will you run another? Why?
